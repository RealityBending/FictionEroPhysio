1. Physiology primer

ECG (electrocardiography) and heart rate

The ECG records the heart's electrical activity through electrodes on the chest. Each heartbeat produces a characteristic wave; its tallest spike is the R-peak. The time between two R-peaks is the RR interval (or NN interval). Heart rate in beats per minute is 60 / RR interval in seconds.

Because a beat only happens about once a second, heart rate is only "measured" at each beat. NeuroKit fills in the values between beats (interpolation) so that ECG_Rate has a value at every sample. That is why a 2-second window only really contains 2–3 independent heart rate values.

What it tells us: heart rate is controlled by both branches of the autonomic nervous system. Emotional and attention-grabbing images typically produce a brief slowing (orienting response) in the first 1–3 s, followed by a speed-up.

HRV (heart rate variability)

The heart does not beat like a metronome: RR intervals vary from beat to beat. HRV indices summarise that variation. Higher beat-to-beat variability generally reflects stronger parasympathetic (vagal, "rest and digest") influence on the heart.

IndexWhat it measures
MeanNNAverage RR interval (the inverse of mean heart rate)
SDNNStandard deviation of RR intervals: overall variability
RMSSDRoot mean square of successive differences: fast, beat-to-beat variability, the most common vagal index
HFPower in the high-frequency band (0.15–0.4 Hz), linked to breathing and vagal activity
LFHFRatio of low- to high-frequency power (its interpretation as "sympathovagal balance" is disputed)
SampEn, HFDComplexity / irregularity of the RR series (sample entropy, Higuchi fractal dimension)
IALS, Ca, AIHeart rate asymmetry: whether accelerations and decelerations contribute differently to variability

Standard guidelines (Task Force, 1996) recommend at least ~5 minutes of recording for short-term HRV. The 8-minute resting state meets this.

Respiration (RSP)

A belt around the lower chest stretches as the participant breathes, giving a wave with one peak per breath. From it we get breathing rate (breaths per minute) and amplitude (depth). Breathing matters for two reasons: it changes with arousal, and it directly affects heart rate (heart rate rises when you breathe in and falls when you breathe out, called respiratory sinus arrhythmia).

EDA (electrodermal activity, skin conductance)

Two electrodes on the fingers measure how well the skin conducts a tiny current, in microsiemens (µS). Sweat glands in the fingers are controlled only by the sympathetic ("fight or flight") nervous system, so EDA is a fairly pure marker of arousal.

  • Tonic component (skin conductance level, SCL): the slow background level, drifting over minutes.
  • Phasic component (skin conductance responses, SCRs): fast bumps on top of it, typically starting 1–3 s after a stimulus and peaking a few seconds later.

EDA is slow, which is why its epochs are longer (up to 8 s after image onset). It also has known problems: some people are "non-responders" (cold hands, little sweating), the gel can dry, and the BITalino sensor maxes out ("clips") at about 25 µS. Several of your session notes mention these.

Sampling rate

The number of measurements per second (Hz). The BITalino records at 1000 Hz and the Muse EEG at 256 Hz. When the streams were combined into one file, everything was resampled to a shared 2000 Hz timeline (twice the highest rate). So your data are at 2000 Hz: 1 second = 2000 samples. Many numbers in the script are written as srate * seconds to convert seconds into samples.

Photosensor (PHOTO channel), events and epochs

The experiment draws a black square in the corner of the screen whenever something important is shown (e.g. an image), and a light sensor taped over that corner records it. The PHOTO channel therefore drops (goes dark) exactly when the image is on screen. This is the most precise way to know when things appeared, because it measures the screen itself rather than when the computer asked for the image.

  • Event: a moment in the recording (here, each image onset and how long it lasted), found from the PHOTO channel.
  • Epoch: a window of signal cut out around an event (e.g. 2 s before to 5 s after each image), with time re-set so that 0 = the event.
  • Baseline correction: subtracting the average of the pre-event part of the epoch, so each trial's response is measured relative to its own starting level.

5. Resting state

Standard 
rs, info = nk.bio_process(ecg=rs["ECG"][0][0], rsp=rs["RSP"][0][0], sampling_rate=srate)

What: NeuroKit's all-in-one processing with its default methods:

  • ECG: filtered to remove slow baseline wander (0.5 Hz high-pass) and mains electrical noise (50 Hz), R-peaks detected, heart rate computed and interpolated between beats → ECG_Clean, ECG_R_Peaks, ECG_Rate.
  • RSP: filtered to the breathing range (about 0.05–3 Hz), breaths detected → RSP_Rate, RSP_Amplitude.

Why: these are well-validated default pipelines; using defaults (and saying so) is standard and reproducible.

Standard Our decision 
hrv = nk.hrv(rs["ECG_R_Peaks"].values.nonzero()[0], sampling_rate=srate)
idx = ["MeanNN", "SDNN", "RMSSD", "SampEn", "HF", "HFD", "LFHF", "IALS", "Ca", "AI"]
hrv.columns = [s + "_RS" for s in hrv.columns]

What: takes the sample positions of all R-peaks, computes NeuroKit's full set of HRV indices, keeps 10 and names them with _RS.

Why: resting HRV from an 8-min recording is standard (it is a trait-like marker of autonomic regulation, often linked to interoception and emotion regulation). Which 10 indices to keep is a choice: they cover the time domain (MeanNN, SDNN, RMSSD), frequency domain (HF, LFHF), complexity (SampEn, HFD) and asymmetry (IALS, Ca, AI).

6. Heartbeat Counting Task

Our decision 
events = nk.events_find(hct["PHOTO"][0][0], threshold_keep="below", duration_min=int(srate * 10))
assert n_events == 6, ...
tolerance = 1.0 if sub == "sub-024" else 0.50
assert max_err < tolerance, ...

What: finds the 6 counting intervals on the photosensor, checks there are exactly 6, and checks each interval's length against the length programmed in the experiment (20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45 s, in random order).

Why: the accuracy score compares the participant's count with the real number of heartbeats in that interval, so the intervals must be right. Checking the photosensor against the behavioural file catches missing or wrong markers before they silently corrupt the scores. The 0.5 s tolerance and the sub-024 exception are our choices. The first 19 participants have no interval lengths saved, so the length check is skipped for them.

Standard 
epochs = nk.epochs_create(hct, events, sampling_rate=srate,
                          epochs_start=0, epochs_end="from_events")
hct_beh["N_R_peaks"] = [epoch["ECG_R_Peaks"].sum() for i, epoch in epochs.items()]
hct_beh["HCT_Accuracy"] = 1 - ((np.abs(hct_beh["HCT_count"] - peaks)) / peaks)

What: cuts out each counting interval exactly (start to end), counts the R-peaks in it (the true number of heartbeats), and computes accuracy for each interval: 1 means perfect, lower means further off.

Why: this is the classic Schandry (1981) heartbeat counting accuracy formula.

Standard 
dfsub["HCT_Accuracy"]    = np.nanmean(hct_beh["HCT_Accuracy"])
dfsub["HCT_Sensibility"] = np.nanmean(hct_beh["HCT_confidence"])
dfsub["HCT_Awareness"]   = scipy.stats.spearmanr(confidence[valid], accuracy[valid]).statistic

What: the three dimensions of interoception from Garfinkel et al. (2015):

  • Accuracy: how close counts are to reality (objective performance).
  • Sensibility: how confident people feel (self-report).
  • Awareness (metacognitive): whether confidence tracks accuracy; here the correlation across the 6 intervals.

Why: the three-dimension model is standard. Using a Spearman correlation for awareness is a common, simple choice; Garfinkel et al. used a ROC-based measure, so worth stating which you use.

To decide

Decide: does 0 mean "felt no heartbeats" (a real answer, keep it) or "did not respond" (exclude it from accuracy too, by moving the replacement above the accuracy line)?

7. Fiction task (Phase 1)

Reminder of one trial: fixation 3 s → label 2 s → fixation 3–5 s (random) → image 4 s → ratings (self-paced). The photosensor only marks the image. Phase 2 has no physiological recording.

Our decision 
fic_beh = fic_beh.sort_values("Trial_Order_Phase1").reset_index(drop=True)
events = nk.events_find(fic["PHOTO"][0][0], threshold_keep="below", duration_min=int(srate * 0.5))
fic_rec = fic_beh[~fic_beh["Trial_Order_Phase1"].isin(list(missing_trials.get(sub, {})))]

What: puts the behavioural rows in the order the images were shown, finds the images on the photosensor, and makes fic_rec, the trials that were actually recorded (all 50, minus any listed in missing_trials).

Why: events and trials are matched by position (1st event = 1st trial). Sorting and removing unrecorded trials keeps that pairing correct, so each physiological response gets the right label and ratings.

Our decision 
assert n_events == len(fic_rec), "Photosensor shows ... images but ... recorded trials expected"
assert max_err < 0.3, "Image duration differs from 4 s by ..."

What: two safety checks: the number of images found must equal the number of recorded trials, and each image must last 4 s (within 0.3 s).

Why: if even one marker were missing, every later trial would be paired with the wrong label. These checks guarantee that can't happen silently. The 0.3 s tolerance was set from the data (sub-001 has one image at 4.20 s, probably a slow screen refresh).

Standard 
fic, info = nk.bio_process(ecg=..., rsp=..., eda=fic["EDA"][0][0], sampling_rate=srate, keep=...)

What: same as resting state, plus EDA: smoothed (low-pass filter) and split into EDA_Tonic (slow level) and EDA_Phasic (fast responses, NeuroKit's default high-pass method), with SCR peaks detected.

Why: separating tonic from phasic is standard EDA practice. Because the slow part is removed from EDA_Phasic, it is already free of slow drift (gel drying etc.).

Standard Our decision 
epochs_ecg = nk.epochs_create(fic, events, sampling_rate=srate, epochs_start=-2, epochs_end=5)
epochs_eda = nk.epochs_create(fic, events, sampling_rate=srate, epochs_start=-2, epochs_end=8)

What: cuts a window around every image: −2 to 5 s for heart rate, −2 to 8 s for EDA and breathing.

Why: epoching around stimulus onset is standard. The windows are our choice:

  • −2 s: a short baseline. It cannot be much longer, because the label appears only 3–5 s before the image.
  • 5 s for ECG: covers the 4 s image plus 1 s, enough for the initial heart rate slowing and speeding up.
  • 8 s for EDA: skin conductance responses start 1–3 s after a stimulus and peak a few seconds later, so the window runs well past the 4 s image.
Standard Our decision 
base_ecg = epoch_ecg[epoch_ecg.index < 0]
resp_ecg = epoch_ecg[epoch_ecg.index >= 0]
"ECG_Rate_Change": resp_ecg["ECG_Rate"].mean() - base_ecg["ECG_Rate"].mean(),
"EDA_Phasic_Max":  resp_eda["EDA_Phasic"].max() - base_eda["EDA_Phasic"].mean(),
...

What: splits each epoch into baseline (before 0) and response (from 0), and computes one number per feature per trial.

Why: subtracting the baseline mean (constant baseline correction) is standard: it removes differences in starting level between trials. We chose to keep constant correction rather than linear detrending (the 2 s baseline holds only 2–3 heartbeats, too few to estimate a slope reliably). The specific features are our choice:
